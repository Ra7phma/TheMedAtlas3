Medical school requirements

A short introduction to each school's teaching style, how each university uses the UCAT, the Situational Judgement Test (SJT), GCSEs and A-levels, plus the lowest UCAT score invited to interview at each school last year (2026 entry), or the published minimum score where that is all a school shares.

New UCAT format. Since 2025 the UCAT has three scored subtests (Verbal Reasoning, Decision Making, Quantitative Reasoning), each 300 to 900, so the total is out of 2700. Abstract Reasoning has been removed. The SJT is reported separately as Band 1 to 4. Scores from before 2025 were out of 3600 and can't be compared directly, so this site only uses figures on the 2700 scale.
Learning styles explained. Integrated: science and clinical medicine are taught together from early on, usually with patient contact in the first year. PBL / case-based: you learn mainly in small groups, working through patient cases and finding out what you need to know. Traditional (science-first): a few years of mostly science-based study before clinical training. These are broad labels, and most schools blend styles. Summaries are brief and courses change, so confirm on each university's course page and at open days.
Please check before you apply. Universities set interview thresholds after applications close, and they change every year. A "lowest invited" score is what the last cohort needed, not a guarantee. A "published minimum" is a cut-off the university states in advance, which is not the same thing as the lowest score actually invited. Where a school uses a combined points score, publishes no minimum, or hasn't released a figure on the new scale, the table says so. Always confirm on each university's own admissions page before choosing your four medicine choices on UCAS.

UCAT resources

Where to practise, learn the test and find advice. Start with the official free material, then add one good question bank if you need more.

Prices, free trials and what's included change often, so check each site before you pay. You don't need to buy every resource, and paid courses are never required to get a good score.

UCAT preparation tips

  • Start with the official site. The real practice tests use the same interface as test day, including the on-screen calculator, so get comfortable with them early.
  • Learn first, then time yourself. Work through each section untimed to understand the question types, then move to timed practice as the test gets closer.
  • Keep an error log. Write down why you got each question wrong (misread, ran out of time, didn't know the method). Patterns show you what to fix.
  • Build a steady routine. Many students prepare for roughly six to eight weeks, a little each day. Start earlier if you have exams or a busy schedule.
  • Don't forget the SJT. It is reported as a band, and many universities reject Band 4. Read the official guidance and the GMC's professional standards to understand what good professional behaviour looks like.
  • Check bursaries and deadlines. Eligible UK students may get help with the test fee. Registration and booking windows are on the official UCAT website.
  • One good question bank is enough. Doing fewer questions and reviewing them properly beats rushing through several banks.

Work experience and volunteering

Medical schools want to see that you understand what a career in healthcare involves and that you have shown care for others. Formal shadowing isn't required, and volunteering and caring roles count just as much.

Eligibility, minimum ages and availability change often. Check each provider's website before applying. Never pay anyone to guarantee you a placement at an NHS site, and remember that paid programmes are not required or favoured by medical schools.

Extracurriculars for a well-rounded application

Medical schools aren't looking for a long list of activities. They want evidence of the qualities good doctors need, such as teamwork, empathy, communication, resilience and curiosity, and proof that you've thought about what you learned.

Quality over quantity. A few activities you enjoy and stick with will do far more for you than a packed CV. Some universities score the personal statement and many don't, but your activities still feed into your interview, so check how each school uses it.
Care and compassion

Caring and volunteering

Regular, long-term roles say more than one-off days.

  • Hospital, hospice or care home volunteering
  • Befriending or charity work
  • Caring for a family member
  • Community or faith-based service
Teamwork and leadership

Working with others

Show what you actually did, not just the title you held.

  • Sports teams, orchestras, choirs or drama groups
  • Scouts, Guides, cadets or the Duke of Edinburgh's Award
  • Running a society or club
  • School council or prefect roles
Communication

Helping others learn and listen

Doctors spend their days explaining things and listening carefully.

  • Peer mentoring or tutoring younger students
  • Debating and public speaking
  • Buddy or transition schemes
  • Customer-facing part-time work
Resilience

Commitment and balance

Sustained effort over years shows stamina, and it helps you stay well.

  • A sport or fitness activity you keep up
  • Music grades or creative projects
  • A part-time job (excellent evidence of responsibility)
  • Learning a language or a craft
Curiosity

Academic interest

Show that you enjoy the science and think critically about it.

  • An Extended Project Qualification (EPQ)
  • Nuffield Research Placements
  • Science or medical society, Biology or Chemistry Olympiad
  • Wider reading, podcasts and free online courses
Practical skills

First aid and life skills

Handy for the real world and a good talking point at interview.

  • First aid training with St John Ambulance or the Red Cross
  • Lifeguard or coaching qualifications
  • Cooking, crafts or hands-on projects
Insight

Understanding healthcare

Show you know what the job and the NHS are really like.

  • Follow NHS and medical news
  • Read about medical ethics and debate it
  • Attend university open days and outreach events
  • Reflect on work experience, including the hard parts

How to make your activities count

  • Reflect as you go. After each activity, write three lines: what you did, what you learned, and how it relates to being a doctor. These notes become your personal statement and interview answers.
  • Go for depth over breadth. Three to five activities you've kept up beat a dozen one-offs.
  • Aim for balance. Try to have something in each area: caring, teamwork, a hobby, and something academic.
  • Keep a log. Record dates, hours, what you did and a contact who can confirm it.
  • Be honest. Interviewers ask follow-up questions, so only write about what you can talk about in detail.
  • Protect your wellbeing. Sleep, rest and time with friends make you a stronger applicant and a better future doctor. Don't overload yourself.
  • Start in Year 12 and keep going. Steady involvement over time looks much better than a rush before the deadline.
